About

My theme for this exhibition is on Filipino heritage, religion, stories, and intersectionality.. With various art pieces such as sculpture, paintings, and even installation. This exhibition considers how artists represent the complexity of Filipino identity while responding to the histories that have shaped the Philippines. The works I have chosen to share and put into my curatorial archive merely don’t represent the culture but reveals on how the culture itself intersects the many paths that leads them.

In my own past history of the Philippines I knew they had been colonized by Spain, the America, and Japan. Spain held reign from 1521 to 1898, followed by American from 1898 to 1946. Japan held territory for only a few years from 1942 to 1945. These eras managed to inflict tremendous influence over Philippines’s architecture, language, religion, government, and social structures. I always ponder as to why my religion is Roman Catholic and what was the previous religion before colonialism.

To answer the question, I delve into the history of the sole proprietor practice. Anitism is an indigenous animist worldview rooted in the belief that spirits inhabit the natural world. Mountains, rivers, trees, and the sky were understood as living presences rather than as empty spaces separate from human life. There has always been this inseparable bond whenever I’m in nature or need to find a way to explain life and reason. It makes sense that anitism was the main religion because for my culture we are deep rooted by a spiritual connection.

The purpose of my exhibition isn’t to reduce Filipino identity to colonization. Colonial history is essential, but it’s not the only story being told. Whenever Philippines is being discussed it’s often represented through political struggles, poverty, national trauma or the legacy of foreign powers. My family moved to California to get away from the same political and economic struggles that transpire in the Philippines. Nevertheless, one can make art of these experiences such as the artists I had selected.

he exhibition is related to my own experience as a Filipino person raised in California. Although I have Filipino heritage, I often feel distant from the traditions and cultural knowledge associated with it. Growing up in America caused me to have so many questions about where I belong and how I should understand my identity. The conflict in which where do I stand? Do I stand with my fellow Filipinos? Or am I too “Americanized”. When people look at me it is obvious I am Asian yet more of a typical Californian who tends to speak like a “valley girl”. Even I try to speak in tagalog it’s obvious that I wasn’t taught it and get looked down upon the Filipino elders.

This exhibition therefore becomes an opportunity for research, reflection, and return. It is a way of looking more closely at the culture I have inherited, while also acknowledging the gaps in my knowledge. The artists in the exhibition provide different entry points into that process. Their works do not simply explain Filipino culture; they demonstrate how it is remembered, questioned, embodied, and continually remade.

Benedicto Cabrera, also known as BenCab, turns social realism, historical critique, and marginalized figures into an enduring visual language for national identity. I find that through his art he invites viewers to confront questions of identity, memory, and social reality. His legacy is not confined to canvas or paper; it lives on through institutions, influence, and a continuing dialogue on what it means to be Filipino in a changing world.

Agnes Arellano is one of the Filipino artists I have heard about and am delighted to discuss about her breathtaking sculptures, especially her works that embody women, infertility, and accentuate the body. There is a common alignment between the two of us which is how religion was a sole importance in our life and in our upbringing.

melda Cajipe Endaya is the first named woman National Artist of the Philippines for Visual Arts and the first woman to receive the distinction in the field. I love the use of colors and representational art that brings attention to certain issues and her not shying away from political struggles is what got me to be fond of her.

Martha Atienza is an outspoken activist who wants to preserve the ecologic environment pertaining mainly to her home of Bantayan Island in the Philippines. As to the reason I have chosen her is because of her outstanding collaboration and connection with the community to bring a better lifestyle for her home. She is passionate despite the hurdles of obstacles she faces from the government.

Anita Magsaysay-Hos challenged the male-dominated art world and used Cubist-inspired forms to represent Filipino life. I selected her because the women in her paintings aren’t just passive or decorative figures. They are active members of their families and communities, shown with strength, dignity, and purpose. Her work connects to my curatorial statement by demonstrating that Filipino identity is shaped not only by colonization and religion, but also by ordinary experiences, cultural traditions, and the resilience of Filipino people.

Ang Kiukok’s work supports my statement by showing how religion is not as separate from social life. Instead it can help people understand the suffering, responsibility, and resilience of people’s daily lives. I grew up in a very religious and sacramental family and community. I went to catholic school when I was young and didn’t like it one bit. However, now I’m grown up I realize I don’t need to be fully devoted but need something to believe in.

Jose Joya is one of my artists that I picked because he shows that Filipino culture can be expressed through color, gesture, atmosphere, and emotion rather than only through recognizable symbols. His impact demonstrates how modern art can remain connected to Filipino place and identity.

Juan Luna is another artist I recognized due to one of his artworks which is the Spoliarium, 1884. He demonstrates how art can become a form of resistance, historical memory, and national pride.

Together these eight artists demonstrates how Filipino art can’t be contained within a single category. The art pieces reveal how they bargain symbols while creating new meanings from them. I know the mindset on old Filipinos, they are stubborn but fully determined to be heard. No matter who reigns over the country, the flame that ignites them doesn’t get put out as fast.

What I hope to get from this exhibition is the beautiful representational artworks that will be showcased and the symbolism of each artist’s works. There were many that surprised me by how they were made and even how long ago some paintings were created. I can tell the influence shifting based on works that were about religion, women’s fertility, or eco system.
