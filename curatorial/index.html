About

My exhibition is about Filipino culture, tradition, religion, memory, and belonging. Bringing together paintings, sculpture, and installation, the exhibition considers how artists represent the complexity of Filipino identity while responding to the histories that have shaped the Philippines. The works gathered here do not present Filipino culture as a single, fixed tradition. Instead it reveals a culture formed through the meeting of indigenous beliefs, colonial influence, religious transformation, personal memory, and everyday experience.

The Philippines has been colonized by Spain, the United States, and Japan. Spain held power from 1521 to 1898, followed by American rule from 1898 to 1946. Japan occupied the Philippines from 1942 to 1945. These periods of foreign rule influenced the country’s architecture, language, religion, government, and social structures. Spanish colonization had one of the most lasting effects, particularly through the introduction and institutionalization of Roman Catholicism. Catholic imagery, rituals, and ideals became deeply embedded in Filipino life, but they also existed alongside older indigenous beliefs and cultural practices.

Before Catholicism became the dominant religion, many Filipino communities practiced anitism, an indigenous animist worldview rooted in the belief that spirits inhabit the natural world. Mountains, rivers, trees, and the sky were understood as living presences rather than as empty spaces separate from human life. This relationship between people, nature, and the spiritual world remains important to understanding the cultural imagination of the Philippines. It also provides a framework for considering the artworks in this exhibition, many of which connect the human body to the land, religious symbolism, labor, birth, suffering, and transformation.

The purpose of my exhibition is not to reduce Filipino identity to colonization. Colonial history is essential to the exhibition, but it is not the only story being told. When the Philippines is discussed it is often represented through political struggles, poverty, national trauma, or the legacy of foreign powers. These realities are part of the country’s history, but they do not encompass the entirety of Filipino life. This exhibition seeks to emphasize the richness, beauty, contradictions, and ordinary experiences that exist beyond those limited representations.

The exhibition is also connected to my own experience as a Filipino person raised in California. Although I have Filipino heritage, I have often felt distant from the traditions and cultural knowledge associated with it. Growing up in America created questions about where I belong and how I should understand my identity. My knowledge for the culture initially consisted of only a few familiar facts: that Spain colonized the Philippines for approximately three hundred years, that Roman Catholicism became the dominant religion, that Spanish and Filipino languages share certain similarities, and that some Filipino surnames are common in Spanish-speaking communities. My own last name, Cruz, is one example of this connection.

This exhibition therefore becomes an opportunity for research, reflection, and return. It is a way of looking more closely at the culture I have inherited, while also acknowledging the gaps in my knowledge. The artists in the exhibition provide different entry points into that process. Their works do not simply explain Filipino culture; they demonstrate how it is remembered, questioned, embodied, and continually remade.

Benedicto Cabrera, also known as BenCab, turns social realism, historical critique, and marginalized figures into an enduring visual language for national identity. I find that through his art he invites viewers to confront questions of identity, memory, and social reality. His legacy is not confined to canvas or paper; it lives on through institutions, influence, and a continuing dialogue on what it means to be Filipino in a changing world.

Agnes Arellano is one of the Filipino artists I have heard about and am delighted to discuss about her breathtaking sculptures, especially her works that embody women, infertility, and accentuate the body. There is a common alignment between the two of us which is how religion was a sole importance in our life and in our upbringing.

Imelda Cajipe Endaya is the first named woman National Artist of the Philippines for Visual Arts and the first woman to receive the distinction in the field. I love the use of colors and representational art that brings attention to certain issues and her not shying away from political struggles is what got me to be fond of her.

Martha Atienza is an outspoken activist who wants to preserve the ecologic environment pertaining mainly to her home of Bantayan Island in the Philippines. As to the reason I have chosen her is because of her outstanding collaboration and connection with the community to bring a better lifestyle for her home. She is passionate despite the hurdles of obstacles she faces from the government.

Anita Magsaysay-Hos challenged the male-dominated art world and used Cubist-inspired forms to represent Filipino life. I selected her because the women in her paintings aren’t just passive or decorative figures. They are active members of their families and communities, shown with strength, dignity, and purpose. Her work connects to my curatorial statement by demonstrating that Filipino identity is shaped not only by colonization and religion, but also by ordinary experiences, cultural traditions, and the resilience of Filipino people.

Ang Kiukok’s work supports my curatorial statement by showing that religion is not separate from social life. Instead, it can help people understand suffering, responsibility, and resilience. His painting also strengthens the exhibition’s focus on the human body as a place where history, faith, and emotional struggle are expressed.

I chose Jose Joya as one of my artists because he shows that Filipino culture can be expressed through color, gesture, atmosphere, and emotion rather than only through recognizable symbols. His impact demonstrates how modern art can remain connected to Filipino place and identity.

Juan Luna is another artist I recognized due to one of his artworks which is the Spoliarium, 1884. He demonstrates how art can become a form of resistance, historical memory, and national pride.

Together, these eight artists demonstrate that Filipino art cannot be contained within a single category. It is at once religious and critical, personal and historical, traditional and contemporary. The works reveal how artists negotiate inherited symbols while creating new meanings from them. Catholic imagery can be used to express devotion, but it can also expose systems of control. The body can represent suffering, but it can also embody resistance and renewal. Daily labor can appear ordinary, yet it can become a record of cultural continuity. The landscape can function as scenery, but it can also hold spiritual, economic, and historical significance.

Mine is ultimately an exhibition about looking more deeply. It asks what is remembered, what has been forgotten, and what it means to recover a cultural identity shaped by both indigenous traditions and foreign influence. It recognizes Filipino culture as layered rather than singular, and as living rather than frozen in the past. Through these artists, the exhibition proposes art as a form of cultural inquiry: a way to encounter history, challenge inherited narratives, and understand the many dimensions of Filipino life.
