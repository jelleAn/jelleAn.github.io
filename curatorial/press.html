Press

Natalie and James Thompson Gallery, San Jose

October 1, 2026 to October 31, 2026

Anjelle Cruz brings together a total of eight works by eight Filipino artists whose practices engage questions of cultural memory, spirituality, colonial history, and everyday life. Moving across painting, sculpture, and installation, the exhibition considers how Filipino identity is shaped through inherited traditions, religious imagery, social experience, and the ongoing recovery of indigenous histories.

The Philippines had been colonized by Spain, America, and Japan. Spanish rule lasted from 1521 to 1898 and had a particularly profound effect on Filipino language, architecture, religion, and cultural practices. Before Catholicism became the dominant religion many Filipino communities practiced Anitism, an indigenous animist tradition grounded in the belief that spirits inhabit mountains, rivers, trees, and the sky.

Rather than presenting colonization as the sole framework through which the Philippines can be understood, Anjelle Cruz emphasizes the richness and complexity of Filipino life. The exhibition is also shaped by a personal investigation of heritage. The recognition that cultural knowledge can become distant for Filipinos raised outside the country and that art can provide a means of returning to, questioning, and understanding that inheritance.

The exhibition includes work by four living artists. Benedicto Cabrera, also known as BenCab, is represented by Sabel, 1966, an oil painting from his well-known Sabel series. The work depicts a woman wrapped in white fabric. It reflects Cabrera’s recurring interest in figures enclosed by swirling, bundled textiles and capes.

Agnes Arellano’s Carcass-Cornucopia, 1987 is a sculpture made from cold-cast marble, unhulled rice, metal hooks, and abaca rope. A woman hangs by her feet while a baby emerges from the womb and rice and organs spill outward. The work brings together images of birth, nourishment, vulnerability, and sacrifice.

Imelda Cajipe Endaya’s Tutol ni Dolorosa, 1991 depicts a woman cloaked in the attire of the Virgin Mary. The painting addresses the imposition of Catholic ideals during Spanish colonization and the erasure of pre-colonial culture associated with that process. “Tutol” means to object or protest; the work’s title can therefore be translated as The Protest of Dolorosa. Endaya is the first woman to receive the distinction of National Artist of the Philippines for Visual Arts.

Martha Atienza is represented by The Coconut Tree Methodology No. 1, 2026 with her exquisite installation to address climate changes, coastal erosion, rising sea levels, and changing societal and fishing structures in the Bantayan Islands of Cebu, Philippines.

The exhibition further includes works by four artists who are no longer living: Anita Magsaysay-Ho, Ang Kiukok, Jose Joya, and Juan Luna. Magsaysay-Ho’s Women Amidst Bananas, 1979 reflects her longstanding interest in women engaged in daily tasks and her incorporation of Cubist influence. The presentation also features Kiukok’s Second Station: Jesus Carries His Cross, 1996, Joya’s Susurro del Mar, 1961, and Luna’s Spoliarium 1884.

Together the works in Anjelle Cruz propose Filipino art as a space where religious traditions, historical violence, indigenous knowledge, and ordinary experience remain in active conversation.
